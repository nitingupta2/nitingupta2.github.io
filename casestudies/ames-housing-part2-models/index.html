Ames Housing - Part 2 - Building Models

Dec 26, 2016 case studies

In a previous post in this series, we did an exploratory data analysis of the Ames Housing dataset.

In this post, we will build both linear and non-linear models and see how well they predict the SalePrice of properties.

Evaluation Criteria

Root-Mean-Squared-Error (RMSE) between the logarithm of the predicted value and the logarithm of the observed SalePrice will be our evaluation criteria. Taking the log ensures that errors in predicting expensive and cheap houses will affect the result equally.

Steps for Building Models

Here are the steps for building models and determining the best hyperparameter combinations by K-fold cross validation:

  • Remove predictors that duplicate the information in other predictors, and encode ordinal features as scores.
  • Partition the training dataset into model training and validation sets. Use stratified sampling such that each partition has a similar distribution of the target variable - SalePrice.
  • Define linear and non-linear models.
  • For each model, create a space-filling (maximum entropy) grid of hyperparameter combinations.
  • For each hyperparameter combination, fit a model on the training set and make predictions on the validation set. Repeat the process for all folds.
  • Determine root mean squared errors (RMSE) and choose the best hyperparameter combination that corresponds to the minimum RMSE.
  • Train each model with its best hyperparameter combination on the entire training set.
  • Calculate RMSE of the each finalized model on the testing set.
  • Finally, choose the best model that gives the least RMSE.

Removing Collinear Predictors

Many features in this dataset describe the same thing in different ways, and some of the features we engineered in the previous post duplicate others. When categorical features are converted to dummy variables, some predictors become exact linear combinations of others. A linear model cannot separate their effects, so its coefficients become unstable or cannot be estimated at all, and lm warns of a “rank-deficient fit”. Tree-based models are not affected by duplicated information, but removing it makes linear models better defined and every model simpler.

Using the association matrix from the previous post, we drop these features because other features already carry their information:

  • HasGarage, HasFireplace and HasMasVnr are the same, or almost the same, as the None category of GarageType, FireplaceQu and MasVnrType.
  • MSSubClass combines the type and the style of a building, which BldgType and HouseStyle already describe. Its duplex category is identical to that of BldgType.
  • TotalBath is exactly FullBath + 0.5 * HalfBath + BsmtBath. We drop HalfBath rather than TotalBath, so we keep the total, which is among the most important features of the tree-based models, and lose no information, because HalfBath can be recovered from the other three.

Encoding Ordinal Features

Features such as OverallQual or KitchenQual have categories that follow a natural order. By default, step_dummy() encodes such an ordered factor with polynomial contrasts: one predictor for each category but one, capturing linear, quadratic, cubic and higher order trends across the categories. Some grades are so rare, such as an OverallCond of 1, that they can be missing from a cross validation fold, and then the highest order contrasts are collinear in that fold.

Instead, we encode each ordered factor as a single integer score that follows the order of its categories, which assumes the grades are equally spaced. Categories that no property has, such as a Poor exterior quality, are dropped first, so the scores are consecutive. Here’s how a linear model fares with each encoding, on the same cross validation folds:

dfEncodingComparison %>% 
  mutate(lm_cv_rmse = round(lm_cv_rmse, 4)) %>% 
  knitr::kable(col.names = c("Encoding of ordinal features", "Predictors", "Folds with a rank-deficient fit", "lm cross validated RMSE"))
Encoding of ordinal features Predictors Folds with a rank-deficient fit lm cross validated RMSE
polynomial contrasts 214 4 0.1470
ordinal scores 151 0 0.1336 
encoding_value <- function(encodingName, column) dfEncodingComparison[[column]][dfEncodingComparison$encoding == encodingName]
  • With polynomial contrasts, lm cannot estimate every coefficient in 4 of the 5 folds. With scores, the fit is rank deficient in 0 folds.
  • Scores cut the number of predictors from 214 to 151, and the cross validated RMSE of lm falls from 0.147 to 0.134. The simpler encoding generalizes better, because the model no longer has to estimate a separate effect for each rare grade.

Finally, step_lincomb() checks for any remaining exact linear combinations among the predictors, such as those that could arise because properties without a basement or a garage share the None category of every basement or garage feature. With scores, it finds none.

After preprocessing, there are 151 predictors, and a linear model can estimate a coefficient for every one of them:

Partitioning Training Data

We split the training data into 5 folds. Within each fold, 80% of the data is used for training models and 20% for validating the predicted values against the actual values.

Let’s look at the distribution of the target variable across all folds:

By using stratified sampling, we ensure that the training and validation distributions of the target variable are similar.

Before creating any new features or indulging in more complex modelling methods, we will cross validate a simple linear model on the training data to establish a benchmark. If more complex approaches do not have a significant improvement in the model validation metrics, then they are not worthwhile to be pursued.

Regularized Linear Model

We will use glmnet that uses LASSO and Ridge Regression with regularization. We will do a grid search of the following hyperparameters that minimize RMSE:

  • penalty: The total amount of regularization in the model.

Non-linear Models

Next, we will train a couple of tree-based algorithms, which are not very sensitive to outliers and skewed data.

ranger (Random Forest)

We use the ranger implementation of random forests, which gives the same results as the randomForest package but runs about 3 times faster. In each ensemble, we have 500 trees (random forest error levels off well before that on a dataset of this size) and do a grid search of the following hyperparameters:

  • mtry: The number of predictors to randomly sample at each split.
  • min_n: The minimum number of data points in a node required to further split the node.

Finalizing Models

For each model, we found the combination of hyperparameters that minimize RMSE. Using those parameters, we can now train the same models on the entire training dataset. Finally, we can use the trained models to predict log(SalePrice) on the entire training set to see the actual v/s predicted log(SalePrice) results.

# Get predicted estimates
get_predicted <- function(model_final, dfNew) {

  # Predict on testing set
  vEstimates <- model_final %>%
    predict(new_data = dfNew) %>%
    pull(.pred)
  
  return(vEstimates)
}

dfResults_train <- dfTrain %>% 
  mutate(actual = log(SalePrice)) %>% 
  select(Id, actual) %>% 
  mutate(glmnet = get_predicted(model_final = model_final_glmnet, dfTrain_Proc),
         ranger = get_predicted(model_final = model_final_rf, dfTrain_Proc),
         xgboost = get_predicted(model_final = model_final_xgboost, dfTrain_Proc))
dfPlot <- dfResults_train %>% 
  gather(model, predicted, -actual, -Id) %>% 
  mutate(model = ordered(model, levels = c("glmnet","ranger","xgboost")))

What’s notable?

  • Both ranger and xgboost models fit the training data closely with the tuned parameters, as the predictions lie close to the straight line drawn at 45 degrees. ranger fits it most closely, with a training RMSE of 0.050 versus 0.096 for xgboost.
  • The glmnet model shows a couple of outliers with Ids 524 and 1299 whose predicted values are far in excess of their actual values. Even properties whose SalePrice is at the lower end, show a wide dispersion in prediced values.
  • The plots label the three largest errors of each model. The properties with Ids 31 and 1299 are each among them for 2 of the 3 models, so they are worth checking for errors in the data.
  • But the true performance can only be measured on unseen testing data.

New Property Premium

Among the top 10 features by importance in our final model, most of the features like square footage, neighborhood and number of bathrooms remain the same throughout the life of the property. Quality and condition of property does change but their evaluation is mostly subjective. The only other feature that cannot be disputed to change over time is PropertyAge.

So, how would the predicted SalePrice differ if a property was newly constructed vis-a-vis the same property if it were constructed more than 30 years earlier, and all the times in between?

We could pick a couple of properties at random, change PropertyAge and see its impact on SalePrice.

getSampleProperty <- function(randomSeed) {
  
  set.seed(randomSeed)
  dfSample <- dfTest %>% 
    filter(PropertyAge >= 30) %>% 
    sample_n(1)
  
  dfSample <- tibble(PropertyAge = 1:dfSample$PropertyAge[1]) %>% expand_grid(dfSample %>% select(-PropertyAge))
}

dfSample <- getSampleProperty(1000) %>% bind_rows(getSampleProperty(2000))

We can see there’s a small premium for a newly constructed property v/s an older property of the same build, quality and condition. This premium isn’t very much in a place like Ames, IA but we’d reckon it would be much higher in a larger metropolitan city.
